## Module 1 — Tom

1. How would you balance attending to Tom’s concerns with the clinic’s scheduling constraints?
2. Discuss the benefits of identifying the type of health assessment most appropriate to the purpose of the encounter. What type of assessment is appropriate for Tom at this visit?

## Module 1 — Nellie

Nellie (she/her), a 74-year-old Indigenous woman, presents to your clinic with her daughter. Nellie lives in a remote Indigenous community and is in the city visiting her daughter. In her home community, she normally receives sporadic, episodic care from an outpost nurse. Her daughter is requesting that Nellie be screened for cancer and diabetes. Nellie speaks very little English and tends to avoid eye contact when questioned.

1. Discuss the process of adding Nellie as a new patient to your clinic.
2. How can the NP provide culturally safe, sensitive care to Nellie and her daughter?
3. How would you address the daughter’s concerns about cancer and diabetes?

## Module 1 — Karen

Karen (she/her), a 78-year-old widow, lives alone in a small, remote Indigenous community. She has had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes for fifteen years. Her two adult children have relocated to an urban area for better employment opportunities. A small, ulcerated area has been present on her right great toe for the past two months, which she has been treating herself. She can reach the nearby town for groceries, supplies, and clinic visits only when a friend can give her a ride. At her last clinic visit six months ago, the new NP suggested that Karen may need to begin insulin therapy to improve her blood glucose control. Karen is reluctant to do so because of the cost of the necessary supplies and a fear of self-injection. She reports that her monthly pension barely covers her groceries and utilities.

1. Based on the case presentation, discuss the social determinants of health that are affecting Karen’s current health status.

2. What eye examination techniques will you include in your assessment?
3. Consider how your physical examination would differ if he had reported eye pain and a sensation of something in his eye.
4. If the NP discovered a steel filing at the 3 o’clock position at the periphery of the iris, what would be the most appropriate next action?

## Module 2 — Beth

4. If the tympanic membrane could not be visualized, how should the NP rule out a middle ear infection?

## Module 2 — Eric

Eric (he/they), an 18-year-old college student, presents to the walk-in clinic with a fever, headache, and sore throat for the past three days. He informs the NP that he has difficulty swallowing, feels fatigued, and has developed a dry cough. He maintains his fluid intake but eats only soup due to difficulty swallowing. He denies any nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea. He presents today because he feels worse than he would with a simple cold.

Examination: 
•    Temperature: 38.2 °C (tympanic).
•    Lymphatic system: bilateral anterior cervical and submental lymphadenopathy, soft, mobile, and tender.
•    HEENT:
•    Nose: erythematous nasal turbinate, no discharge.
•    Throat: inflamed pharynx.
•    Tonsils: greyish-white exudate.
•    The remainder of the examination is unremarkable.

1. At this stage, list the differential diagnoses the NP should consider based on Eric’s presenting symptoms. Provide your rationale.
2. What is the total score using the Sore Throat Score?
3. How predictive of a GAS infection are this client’s signs and symptoms?
4. What complications are you concerned about, and what is the risk of these if treatment is delayed?
5. Based on the clinical findings, what diagnostic investigations, if any, should the NP order? Provide your rationale.
6. If the NP had noted splenomegaly during the physical examination, would this have changed the diagnostic reasoning?

## Module 3 — Martha

## Module 3 — Reid

Reid (he/him), a healthy 26-year-old Black man, presents with a "rash" on his neck that he noticed yesterday. He initially thought it was razor burn, which he had experienced before, but he came in today because his razor burn had never been "crusty" before. After looking up his symptoms online, he became worried that he might have something more serious.

After a more thorough assessment, the NP learns that Reid's symptoms began yesterday after shaving and that they have occurred in the past, always triggered by shaving. He is self-conscious about the scarring developing on his neck. He feels this episode is different, however, because of marked redness and crusting around some of the lesions on his left lower neck.

Reid takes no medications. He tries to minimize shaving when his razor burn is particularly bothersome and has previously used benzoyl peroxide without relief. He has no allergies.

A focused examination of Reid's neck reveals scarring within the beard area. There are numerous papules and pustules at various stages of healing. On the left lower neck, there is mild redness with numerous red, inflamed papules and open pustules accompanied by obvious yellow crusting.

1. What are some initial differential diagnoses?
2. What are the key health history questions to help rule these initial differentials in or out?
3. What is the most likely diagnosis, and why?

## Module 4 — Ted

He also has pain in his right foot that makes walking difficult, and a small ulcer on the sole of that foot. Ted is single and a known type 2 diabetic, with an HbA1c of 0.081 (8.1%, indicating poor control), a BMI of 35, and a 25-year history of smoking one pack of cigarettes per day.

1. What key health history questions should the NP ask Ted during the history?
2. What differential diagnoses can you make based on Ted’s symptoms? What are the key elements (history and physical examination) that distinguish each?
3. If Ted reports increased pain at night and calf cramping while walking, what would your differential diagnosis be?
4. What criteria would indicate that his wound is infected?

## Module 4 — Mrs. Tremblay

Mrs. Tremblay (she/her), 42 years old, has a pale complexion and spends much of her annual vacation in the sun. She consults the NP because she has noticed growth and a change in colour of a mole on her leg. The NP removes the adhesive bandage covering the lesion on her lower leg. The examination reveals an 8 mm, dark-pink lesion with central erosion; the borders are raised and irregular.

1. As the NP examines the lesion, how would they explain to Mrs. Tremblay the changes that may indicate a malignant melanoma?
2. What evidence exists regarding the accuracy of skin examination in screening for melanoma?
3. Differentiate among the types of pathological conditions the lesion may represent.
4. If a pathological condition is present, what diagnostic investigations would be required?

## Module 4 — Iris

Iris (she/her), 68 years old, was admitted to the family medicine unit approximately 12 hours ago with pyelonephritis. During rounds, Iris's primary RPN informs you that her Braden Risk Assessment score was 12 (Sensory Perception 3, Moisture 2, Activity 1, Mobility 2, Nutrition 3, Friction and Shear 1). Her skin is quite moist owing to intermittent fevers. Iris reports pain at her coccyx and is having difficulty repositioning because of weakness.

Iris is of Indo-Caribbean origin and has a darker skin tone, which can make erythema assessment more challenging, as darker pigmentation may mask redness.

Despite a diligent prevention strategy, Iris's nurse calls you to assess her coccyx. Over the bony prominence of the sacrum, you observe a shallow, open ulcer with a reddish-pink wound bed, with no slough or bruising present.

1. What is the significance of Iris’s Braden Risk Assessment score?
2. What are some key risk factors for the development of pressure injuries?
3. What assessments could the NP perform that might indicate a pressure-related injury when erythema is harder to see in darker skin?
4. What is the most likely diagnosis for the shallow sacral ulcer described?

He reports that members of his family live into their nineties, and he denies following any particular diet or exercise program. His fasting lipid profile reveals total cholesterol 6.1 mmol/L; triglycerides 3.7 mmol/L; HDL 0.6 mmol/L; LDL 4.65 mmol/L; and a TC/HDL ratio of 10.1.

3. What questions should the NP ask this client about his food choices to better understand his triglyceride and LDL levels?
